# User Research Protocol 

## The Story’s Target Audience

My story’s target audience are community advocates and policymakers who want to better understand the current rise in homelessness, and who want to be involved in systemic change that properly addresses the issue. 

## Identifying Individuals to Interview 

Going to a policy school gives me access to a range of passionate individuals, both at my school and in my wider network, who care about advocating for and implementing policies that justly address the biggest problems facing society, including homelessness. These students are also very data-oriented, making them a prime audience for my story which aims to couple data with real human stories to inspire change. As such, I can interview a variety of my peers, who will be the advocates and policymakers of tomorrow. 

## My Goals and What I Hope to Learn

By interviewing my peers I hope to learn if my story gives them a better understanding of the recent surge in homelessness our country is facing, and if the story flows logically and is clear. I want to know what aspects confuse them and what aspects are particularly memorable. I also want to understand what aspects were most persuasive. I am also curious if all elements of my story flow seamlessly into my conclusion/call-to-action. Because of my peers’ data backgrounds, I also hope to learn if my data visualizations complement my story. I also hope to gain insight on how to improve the aesthetic appearance of my story. 

## The Interview Script: Questions to Ask
1. What was your main takeaway from my story? What did you learn from it?

2. What aspect(s) of the story stood out the most to you/were the most memorable? 

3. Which aspect(s) of the story, if any, did you find to be especially persuasive?

4. What aspect(s) of the story were confusing?

5. Is my story’s progression logical? Do the visualizations, statistics, personal stories, ideas, and conclusions naturally flow and connect, or were there points of disconnect? Are my conclusions supported by the rest of the story?

6. How did the data visualizations help or hurt your overall understanding of the story? Were there particular visualizations or aspects of the visualizations that seemed especially useful, or confusing?

7. How might you improve the visual elements (background photos, color, text font, color, style, etc) of the story to keep it minimalistic, but also aesthetically pleasing and engaging?

8. How else might you improve upon the story and the way it is presented here? 


## Interview findings

### Respondent 1, MSPPM Data-Analytics Student

1. Main Takeaway: Homelessness is still alive and well in America and legislation is not making it any easier for this population to achieve housing and we must take steps to encourage law makers to do better.
2. Most Memorable Aspect: The personal stories. 
3. Most Persuasive Aspect: The personal stories, especially the story with Jennifer, which showed how disability and homelessness interact with one another. 
4. Most Confusing Aspect: The story was very clear.
5. Story Progression: The progression was logical and the different elements connected.
6. Data Visualizations: Liked the map, and liked seeing the dips in the line charts in 2021.
7. Improving Visual Elements: Liked the color palette, it fit the somber mood of the topic. Be careful of white text on light backgrounds, and maybe add a video or moving picture element to boost engagement.
8. Other Areas of Improvement: Could allude to the personal stories in the subtitle since they play a key role in the story. Could also find stories about queer homelessness to add another dimension. Could also include resources such as information about Housing First, or direction to the Invisible People organization (where the personal stories came from)

### Respondent 2, Master of Public and International Affairs Student

1. Main Takeaway: Homelessness has been increasing and there are so many stories and real lives impacted.
2. Most Memorable Aspect: The stories in general stood out and conveyed the urgency of the situation, and Jennifer’s story was most memorable.
3. Most Persuasive Aspect: Approaching this topic from a human rights perspective was very persuasive.
4. Most Confusing Aspect: The story was very clear.
5. Story Progression: The story flowed well. 
6. Data Visualizations: The visualizations were very clear and their simplicity makes them accessible to any audience.
7. Improving Visual Elements: The transition from the background photo of SCOTUS to the hand on the chainlink fence was very powerful, so maybe letting that stand on its own before text would make it even more impactful. The big text was also very impactful so maybe include more of it.
8. Other Areas of Improvement: Towards the end as the story builds up to the conclusion it might be good to shorten some of the stories, since the audience likely already understands the stakes by that point and is ready to learn what can be done.

### Respondent 3, MSPPM Data Analytics Student

1. Main Takeaway: Homelessness is more than just a number, it is about the lived experiences of the people in it, and the data of homelessness only reflects a single night, so the issue is even larger than we think.
2. Most Memorable Aspect: The stories stood out and highlighted how homelessness becomes a cycle that traps people in it. Craig’s story about the citations impeding future employment especially stood out.
3. Most Persuasive Aspect: The graphs, especially the ones showing the increase over time, were very persuasive in showing this issue is bigger than just a single number.
4. Most Confusing Aspect: The story was not confusing.
5. Story Progression: The story had good transitions and everything was tied together well at the end. The story was easy to read and understand. It was simple, but not too simple given the stakes of the subject matter. 
6. Data Visualizations: The visualizations were simple and straightforward. They were immediately understandable. 
7. Improving Visual Elements: The black and white really matched the tone of the story, and it was not too distracting.
8. Other Areas of Improvement: The map could be made stronger if instead of comparing raw counts, it compared the percentage of homeless residents compared to overall residents.

### Respondent 4, MSPPM Flagship Student

1. Main Takeaway: The covid-19 pandemic effect on homelessness and on collecting homelessness data is very clear in this story; also learned the difference between sheltered and unsheltered homelessness. .
2. Most Memorable Aspect: Tying these numbers for specific people’s lived experiences really brought the point home. Introducing relevant policy was very interesting and helped respondent to understand what criminalization policies look like in practice  
3. Most Persuasive Aspect: Introducing relevant policy was very interesting and helped understand what criminalization policies look like in practice. The call to action was especially clear.  
4. Most Confusing Aspect: While they understand that covid-19 affected data collection, could there have also been widespread efforts to tackle homelessness that also led to a massive reduction? They know there is talk of Allegheny County’s 2020 homelessness counts being low because of widespread action.  
5. Story Progression: The story flow is very logical. Starting out with a number and a personal story then continuing into more statistics based work flowed perfectly into each other. 
6. Data Visualizations: The visualizations were easy to understand.
7. Improving Visual Elements: Loved the use of greyscale to set a somber mood. Maybe to make the numbers bolded in sentences more impactful they could be given a different color to really get them to jump off the page
8. Other Areas of Improvement: Beyond the color for numbers and the possible research into effects of Covid-19 homelessness action which could add an interesting aspect of the effect of intentional action, this story does not need much else.

### Respondent 5, Master of Engineering Public Policy Student

1. Main Takeaway: Homelessness is not societally viewed the way it should be, on a more personal level; policymakers do not think of these lived experiences when legislating.
2. Most Memorable Aspect: The lack of color really stood out. 
3. Most Persuasive Aspect:  The ending and call to action were very strong. 
4. Most Confusing Aspect:  The first visualization was not necessarily confusing, but felt starting at 2014 wasn’t completely necessary. The jump could be more clear from a plus minus bar chart. 
5. Story Progression: Two stories in a row could benefit from a transition to make them flow more smoothly. 
6. Data Visualizations: The visualizations don’t necessarily fit the aesthetic, and are very plain in terms of styling and colors. There could be opportunities to use color to highlight. The map also doesn’t work as well with pure numbers, but would be improved with percentages (and might still require some sort of explanation because California and New York might still be so stark). Turn the statistic about Black Americans into a small visualization 
7. Improving Visual Elements: Utilize the grey color scheme (which was good) to the story's advantage more, adding color to important numbers, or at the end, just somewhere impactful. So data visualization backgrounds might need to be grey, too. Making the backgrounds more related to the stories could help (for example, snow in the background for Craig’s story, but the chainlink fence image was powerful too, so keep it somewhere).
8. Other Areas of Improvement: At a point the personal stories kept going on and felt more like a laundry list, could benefit from a bigger break between stories.

# Synthesizing the Interviews and Identifying Changes for Part III 

My respondents identified very similar strengths and weaknesses, which will help me identify areas for improvement.

Over all the interviews, the personal stories stood out, and they were highly persuasive and impactful, so I will maintain these as a key aspect of my story. As suggested, I might include a visual element from the Invisible People organization (where the stories came from), or at least will make it clear that is where the stories originated from. This will give the audience more context as to who the people I am referring to are, and gives the audience a chance to explore other stories if they wish. Similarly, as suggested, I might include other resources for audience members to explore, such as information on the Housing Not Handcuffs organization I cited information from.

Two respondents did note, however, that by the end, maybe there were too many stories. So I might see where I can condense them down.

Overall, the story’s progression was also strong for my respondents. One did suggest a few places for transitions (such as between back-to-back stories), which I do think is a good idea. 

The respondents enjoyed the grey scale, and suggested utilizing bursts of color for heavy impact and emphasis, so I will go through and adjust accordingly. Two respondents also found the photo of the chainlink fence to be especially powerful, so I might find a way to make that stand on its own more. 

Two respondents also suggested adjusting the map visualization to show percentages of people experiencing homelessness rather than the raw counts to make California and New York not stand out as much, when that is not the point of the visualization.
